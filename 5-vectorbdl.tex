What we have seen in the previous chapter is our first example of vector bundle, which is just a way to call a vector space depending continuously (or smoothly) on some parameters, for example points on a manifold.

This concept can be useful for example to extend the meaning of vector-valued functions to the manifold setting.
We saw that vector fields assign to each $p\in M$ a vector in the space $T_pM$ which varies with $p$. Vector bundles are a way to do the same, but mapping the point to some vector space $E_p$ which also varies with $p$.
As we will see, their \emph{section}s are a way to assign a vector $s(p)\in E_p$ to every point, akin to what we did with vector fields, and, indeed, the construction is done in such a way that, locally, these sections look like ordinary vector-valued functions.
Where this gets particularly interesting is investigating the global properties of the construction, though what I mean would become cleared only in more advanced courses.

This may seem to be just a pointless exercise, but vector bundles (and their generalizations), pop up in very disparate parts of mathematics. See for instance \href{https://mathoverflow.net/questions/7836/why-is-it-useful-to-study-vector-bundles}{this MathOverflow answer} or \cite{Zakharevich2019Attitudes}. They are also common in physics, for instance in gauge theory, where the gauge field is a connection on a principal bundle, and the matter fields are sections of associated vector bundles, and solid-state physics.

Let's briefly look at a particular case of the latter example. In a periodic crystal, the states of an electron depend on a parameter $k$, called \emph{quasi-momentum}, which in three dimensions lives on a torus $\bT^3$, the so-called \emph{Brillouin zone}.
The possible energies for the electron are given by functions $\varepsilon_n(k)$, whose images while varying $k$ are called \emph{energy bands}.
There is a special energy, that we will denote $E_F$, called the \emph{Fermi energy}, which in some sense informs physicists about the insulating or metallic properties of the crystal\footnote{Don't worry if you don't know what it means, it is just a way to say that they determine if the crystal is an electrical insulator or if it allows electrons to travel freely through it.}.
If we fix one of the bands, its contribution to the \emph{Fermi surface} is the level set
\begin{equation}
	\Sigma = \{k\in\bT^3 : \varepsilon_n(k)=E_F\},
\end{equation}
where $E_F$ is the energy separating occupied from unoccupied states at zero temperature.
We are going very fast here, don't worry too much about the physical details, but if you want to get a glimpse to the physics behind this, you can have a look at \cite{Dugdale_2016} or dig out a book on topological insulators from your university library.

The space above is something we already know how to handle: if $E_F$ is a regular value of $\varepsilon_n$, then $\Sigma$ is a smooth surface.

So, where is the vector bundle? Assuming that the band is nondegenerate and stays separated from the other bands near $\Sigma$, the corresponding electron states at each $k$ span a one-dimensional complex vector space $L_k$.
So we have again a vector space moving with a point on a manifold, only this time the vectors are quantum states rather than tangent vectors, and the manifold is the Fermi surface.\footnote{Here the vector spaces are complex rather than real. We will give the definition for real vector spaces, but the construction is practically the same if we replace $\R$ by $\mathbb{C}$.}

Locally we can choose a normalized state $u(k)$ depending smoothly on $k$, and use it as a basis for $L_k$.
On the overlap of two different charts, the two choices of state differ by a phase $e^{i\theta(k)}$, which we can think of as a change of basis in a one-dimensional complex vector space.
Now, it is tempting to think that we could keep adjusting these phases until we have a smooth choice of basis on the whole surface... but this is not always possible!

For example, a Fermi surface surrounding a \emph{Weyl point}, a particular crossing between energy bands, can carry a bundle for which there is no such global choice\footnote{The obstruction in this example is measured by an integer called the \emph{first Chern number}, proportional to the flux of the \emph{Berry curvature} through the surface. A nonzero value rules out a global smooth choice of normalized eigenstate. In Appendix~\ref{sec:first-chern-number} we will concisely define this number and explain its relation to Berry curvature. For more details see for instance the \href{https://web.math.princeton.edu/~js129/PDFs/paris_top_insul_july_2022.pdf}{Paris Lectures on Topological Insulators by J.~Shapiro}.}.

Understanding this example would take us well beyond the scope of these notes, but the point is the same as before: knowing each vector space separately does not tell us how they fit together, and this extra information can have consequences, in this case even for the electrical properties of a material.

\section{Vector bundles}\label{sec:vec-bdls}

\begin{definition}\label{def:vector_bundle}\idxdef{Vector bundle}\idxdef{Trivial bundle}\idxdef{Trivialization}
	A \emph{vector bundle of rank $r$} over a manifold $M$ is a manifold $E$ together with a smooth surjective map $\pi : E \to M$ such that, for all $p\in M$, the following properties hold:
	\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
		\item the \emph{fibre over $p$}, $E_p := \pi^{-1}(p)$, has the structure of vector space of dimension $r$;
		\item there is a neighbourhood $U\subset M$ of $p$ and a diffeomorphism $\varphi: \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times \R^r$ such that
		      \begin{enumerate}
			      \item $\pi_1 \circ \varphi = \pi$ where $\pi_1: U\times\R^r\to U$ is the projection on the first factor,
			      \item for all $q\in U$, $\varphi\big|_{E_q} : E_q \to \{q\}\times \R^r$ is an isomorphism of vector spaces.
		      \end{enumerate}
	\end{enumerate}

	The space $E$ is called the \emph{total space}, the manifold $M$ is the \emph{base space}, $\pi$ its projection and each of the maps $\varphi$ is called \emph{local trivialization}.

	If there exists a trivialization defined on the whole manifold, that is a map $\varphi: E \to M\times \R^r$, such map is called \emph{global trivialization}, the vector bundle is said to be \emph{trivializable} and the bundle $(E, \varphi)$ itself is called \emph{trivial} bundle.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}\label{ex:simple_bundles}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Perhaps the simplest example of vector bundle is the trivial vector bundle of rank $1$, often called \emph{trivial line bundle}, $E = M \times \R$ with projection on the first component $\pi_1: E \to M$.
		\item In the same spirit, a trivial vector bundle of rank $r$ over a manifold $M$ is the product space $E = M\times \R^r$ with the projection on the first component $\pi_1: E\to M$.
		\item The tangent bundle $TM$ with its projection to the base $\pi:TM\to M$ is a vector bundle\footnote{Exercise: prove the statement constructing the local diffeomorphisms.}.
		      In this case the fibres are the tangent spaces $\pi^{-1}(p) = T_pM$.
		      If the tangent bundle of a manifold is trivialisable, then its base manifold is said to be \emph{parallelisable}.
		\item If $\pi_i: E_i\to M_i$, $i=1,2$, are vector bundles, then $\pi = (\pi_1, \pi_2): E_1\times E_2 \to M_1\times M_2$ is another vector bundle, called the \emph{product bundle}, whose fibres are the product of the fibres of the two original bundles.
		      A particular example of this is the tangent bundle $T(M_1\times M_2)$, which is diffeomorphic to $TM_1 \times TM_2$.
		\item Other examples will appear throughout the course.
	\end{itemize}
\end{example}

\end{exercise}

\begin{remark}
	In the spirit of getting rid of redundant information and making the language more concise, it is common to refer to a vector bundle $(E, \pi : E \to M)$ only referring to the bundle projection: $\pi : E \to M$.
\end{remark}

\end{exercise}

\begin{remark}
	Let $\pi:E \to M$ be a vector bundle of rank $r$.
	Assume that $E$ itself is the base space of another vector bundle $\pi_1: E_1\to E$ of rank $s$.
	It is tempting to assume that the \emph{composite} $\pi\circ\pi_1: E_1 \to M$ is a vector bundle of rank $r+s$ over $M$, perhaps interestingly, this would lead us off-track: the local trivializations of $E$ and $E_1$, in general, do not even give a vector space structure on the fibers $(\pi\circ\pi_1)^{-1}(p)$.

	A particular example of this is the tangent bundle of the tangent bundle: if $M$ is a $n$-manifold, its tangent bundle $TM$ is a $2n$-manifold, and its tangent bundle $T(TM)$ is a $4n$-manifold.
	Repeating the construction in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:tgbdlsmoothmfld} with the charts $(\pi^{-1}(U),\widetilde\varphi)$ of $TM$, we obtain charts of $T(TM)$ with coordinates $(x,v,\xi,\eta)\in\varphi(U)\times\R^n\times\R^n\times\R^n$.
	If $\sigma := \psi\circ\varphi^{-1}$ is the transition map between two charts of $M$, the transition map of $TM$ is $(x,v)\mapsto(\sigma(x), D\sigma(x)v)$ and, differentiating it once more, the one of $T(TM)$ is
	\begin{equation}
		(x,v,\xi,\eta)\mapsto \big(\sigma(x),\, D\sigma(x)v,\, D\sigma(x)\xi,\, D\sigma(x)\eta + D^2\sigma(x)(\xi,v)\big),
	\end{equation}
	where $D^2\sigma(x)(\xi,v)$ denotes the vector with components $\frac{\partial^2\sigma^i}{\partial x^j\partial x^k}(x)\,\xi^j v^k$.
	For fixed $x$, the map $(v,\xi,\eta)\mapsto\big(D\sigma(x)v, D\sigma(x)\xi, D\sigma(x)\eta + D^2\sigma(x)(\xi,v)\big)$ is not linear\footnote{The appearance of second derivatives of the transition maps is a first glimpse of the \emph{jet bundles}, geometric structures that keep track in an invariant way of the Taylor polynomials of functions (or sections) up to some fixed order.
	The simplest one is the \emph{$1$-jet bundle} $J^1M = \R\times T^*M$ of a manifold $M$, built out of the cotangent bundle that we will meet in the next chapter.
	Jet bundles, together with their higher-order versions, provide a natural setting for the study of partial differential equations on manifolds and are often playing an important role, for instance, in geometric mechanics. We will not discuss them further here, but for a first and brief encounter with these ideas you can refer to~\cite[Chapter 22]{book:lee}.} as soon as $D^2\sigma(x)\neq0$: for example, if $\sigma(x) = (x^1+(x^1)^2, x^2, \ldots, x^n)$, the first component of the last block is $(1+2x^1)\eta^1 + 2\xi^1v^1$.
	So, sadly, these charts do not make $T(TM)$ into a vector bundle of rank $3n$ over $M$.

	They are however an example of a more general structure, the \emph{fiber bundle}.
	A smooth fiber bundle with \emph{typical fiber} a manifold $F$ is a smooth surjective map $\pi:E\to M$ such that each $p\in M$ has a neighbourhood $U$ with a diffeomorphism $\varPhi:\pi^{-1}(U)\to U\times F$ satisfying $\operatorname{pr}_U\circ\varPhi = \pi$, where $\operatorname{pr}_U:U\times F\to U$ is the projection on the first factor.
	On overlaps, the transition maps take the form $\varPhi_\alpha\circ\varPhi_\beta^{-1}(p,f) = (p, \tau_{\alpha\beta}(p)(f))$ with $\tau_{\alpha\beta}(p)\in\diff(F)$.
	Vector bundles are the fiber bundles with $F=\R^r$ whose local trivializations are linear on the fibers, so that $\tau_{\alpha\beta}(p)\in GL(r,\R)$.

	In the case of $T(TM)$, we have $(\pi\circ\pi_1)^{-1}(U) = T(\pi^{-1}(U))$, and the chart of $T(TM)$ constructed above followed by $\varphi^{-1}\times\id_{\R^{3n}}$ is a diffeomorphism $(\pi\circ\pi_1)^{-1}(U)\to U\times\R^{3n}$ compatible with the projections on $U$.
	The corresponding $\tau_{\alpha\beta}$ are the maps of $\R^{3n}$ written above, which are diffeomorphisms with inverse
	\begin{equation}
		(w,\zeta,\theta)\mapsto\big(Aw,\, A\zeta,\, A\big(\theta - D^2\sigma(x)(A\zeta,Aw)\big)\big),
		\qquad A := D\sigma(x)^{-1}.
	\end{equation}
	Thus $\pi\circ\pi_1: T(TM)\to M$ is a fiber bundle with typical fiber $\R^{3n}$.
\end{remark}

To compare vector bundles it is useful to define the following concept.
\begin{definition}\idxdef{Isomorphism of vector bundles}
	An \emph{isomorphism} between two vector bundles $\pi_i: E_i \to M$, $i=1,2$, over the same base space $M$ is a homeomorphism $h:E_1 \to E_2$ which maps every fiber $\pi_1^{-1}(p)$ to the corresponding fiber $\pi_2^{-1}(p)$ by a linear isomorphism.
	The map $h$ is a \emph{diffeomorphism} between vector bundles if it is a diffeomorphism which maps fibres to fibres isomorphically.
	In this case we say that the vector bundles are \emph{diffeomorphic}.
\end{definition}

Since an isomorphism preserves all the structure of a vector bundle, isomorphic bundles are often regarded as the same.

Of course, we can also define subbundles.

\begin{definition}\idxdef{Subbundle}
	Let $\pi:E \to M$ be a rank-$r$ vector bundle and $F\subset E$ a submanifold.
	If for all $p\in M$, the intersection $F_p := F\cap E_p$ is a $k$-dimensional subspace of the vector space $E_p$ and $\pi|_F : F \to M$ defines a rank-$k$ vector bundle, then $\pi|_F: F \to M$ is called a \emph{subbundle} of $E$.
\end{definition}

All the examples given so far are nice and well, but how can we construct these vector bundles?
The first idea that may come to mind, is to  try and mimic the construction we gave of the tangent bundle of a smooth manifold: provided a collection of vector spaces for each point of the manifolds, we need to construct a suitable topology for the whole space, equip the disjoint union of vector spaces with a smooth structure and then construct local trivializations that are compatible with such structure.
This may sound quite a mouthful, but turns out to be the right approach.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:bundle_chart_thm}
	Let $M$ be a smooth $n$-manifold without boundary and let $E_p$ be a given $k$-dimensional real vector space for each $p\in M$.
	Define
	\begin{equation}
		E := \bigsqcup_{p\in M} E_p, \qquad
		\pi : E\to M \mbox{ such that } \pi : v_p \in E_p \mapsto p,
	\end{equation}
	and assume there exists an open cover $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A}$ of $M$ for which the following holds
	\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
		\item for each $\alpha\in A$, there exists a bijection $\varPhi_\alpha: \pi^{-1}(U_\alpha) \to U_\alpha\times\R^k$ whose restriction $\varPhi_\alpha|_{E_p} : E_p \to \{p\}\times\R^k \sim \R^k$ is a linear isomorphism;
		\item for each $\alpha,\beta \in A$ with $U_{\alpha\beta}:=U_\alpha \cap U_\beta \neq \emptyset$, there exists a matrix-valued smooth map $\tau_{\alpha\beta}: U_{\alpha\beta} \to GL(k,\R)$ such that\footnote{Here $\tau_{\alpha\beta}(p) v$ denotes the usual product of the $k\times k$ matrix $\tau_{\alpha\beta}(p)$ with the vector $v\in\R^k$.}
		      \begin{align}
			      \varPhi_\alpha \circ \varPhi_\beta^{-1} : U_{\alpha\beta}\times\R^k & \to U_{\alpha\beta}\times\R^k         \\
			      (p, v)                                                              & \mapsto (p, \tau_{\alpha\beta}(p) v).
		      \end{align}
	\end{enumerate}
	Then $E$ has a unique topology and a smooth structure making it into a smooth manifold without boundary and a smooth rank $k$ vector bundle over $M$ with $\pi$ as its projection and $\{(U_\alpha, \varPhi_\alpha)\}$ as smooth local trivializations.
\end{theorem}
The maps $\tau_{\alpha\beta}$ are called \emph{transition function}s between the local trivializations.
\begin{proof}
	\newthought{Part 1. $E$ has a structure of smooth manifold}.
	We need to use charts of $M$ to define charts from some neighbourhood of fibers $E_p$ to $\R^n\times\R^k$.
	For each $p\in M$, choose some $\alpha\in A$ with $p\in U_\alpha$ and a smooth chart $(V_p, \varphi_p)$ of $M$ such that $p\in V_p \subseteq U_\alpha$.
	Observe that $E_p\subseteq\pi^{-1}(V_p) \subseteq \pi^{-1}(U_\alpha)$ and $\widetilde{V}_p := \varphi_p(V_p) \subseteq \R^n$ is open, and therefore it may be natural to consider the collection $\{(\pi^{-1}(V_p), \widetilde{\varphi}_p)\}$ of all the charts obtained in this way, for all $p\in M$ and all possible choices of $\alpha$ and $(V_p,\varphi_p)$, where
	\begin{equation}
		\widetilde{\varphi}_p := (\varphi_p\times \id_k) \circ \varPhi_\alpha : \pi^{-1}(V_p) \xrightarrow{\varPhi_\alpha} V_p\times\R^k \xrightarrow{\varphi_p\times \id_k} \widetilde{V}_p\times \R^k,
	\end{equation}
	as a candidate to apply the Smooth Manifold Lemma~\ref{lem:manifold_chart}.
	This would give $E$ both a topology and a smooth structure.

	Checking the requirements for the Smooth Manifold Lemma is then relatively straightforward.
	First of all, observe that $\widetilde{\varphi}_p$ is a bijection from $\pi^{-1}(V_p)$ onto $\widetilde{V}_p\times\R^k \subseteq \R^{n+k}$ since it is the composition of bijective maps.
	By construction, for $p,q\in M$, we have
	\begin{equation}
		\widetilde{\varphi}_p(\pi^{-1}(V_p) \cap \pi^{-1}(V_q)) = \varphi_p(V_p\cap V_q)\times \R^k
	\end{equation}
	which is open in $\R^{n+k}$ since $\varphi_p$ is a homeomorphism onto an open $\widetilde{V}_p\subseteq\R^n$.
	Finally, on the overlap of two charts, if $\alpha, \beta\in A$ are the indices chosen for $\widetilde{\varphi}_p$ and $\widetilde{\varphi}_q$ respectively, we have
	\begin{equation}
		\widetilde{\varphi}_p \circ \widetilde{\varphi}_q^{-1} =
		(\varphi_p \times \id_k) \circ \varPhi_\alpha \circ \varPhi_\beta^{-1} \circ (\varphi_q \times \id_k)^{-1}.
	\end{equation}

	At this point, though, $E$ has no topology yet and the maps $\varPhi_\alpha$ are just bijections. However, we still have hypothesis (ii) in our toolbox, which is exactly what we need: for $(x,v)\in\varphi_q(V_p\cap V_q)\times\R^k$,
	\begin{equation}
		\widetilde{\varphi}_p \circ \widetilde{\varphi}_q^{-1}(x,v) = \left(\varphi_p\circ\varphi_q^{-1}(x),\ \tau_{\alpha\beta}\big(\varphi_q^{-1}(x)\big)v\right),
	\end{equation}
	and it is smooth, since $\varphi_p\circ\varphi_q^{-1}$ and $\tau_{\alpha\beta}\circ\varphi_q^{-1}$ are smooth.
	Therefore properties (i) and (ii) of the Smooth Manifold Lemma are satisfied.

	Property (iii) of the Smooth Manifold Lemma follows from the fact that $M$ is a manifold and thus the open cover $\{V_p | p \in M\}$ has a countable subcover.

	We are left with the Hausdorff property, that is, property (iv) of the Smooth Manifold Lemma.
	This can be checked in the same vein as we did for the analogous proof in the case of the tangent bundle:
	if two vectors belong to the same space $E_p$, then they belong to one of the charts we have constructed and we can separate them taking the preimages of two disjoint open neighbourhoods of $\R^k$ containing them; if the two points belong to two distinct spaces $E_p$ and $E_q$, $p\neq q$, we can pick two disjoint neighbourhoods $V_p$ and $V_q$ so that their preimages $\pi^{-1}(V_p)$ and $\pi^{-1}(V_q)$ are disjoint coordinate neighbourhoods separating the two points.

	With this property also satisfied, Lemma~\ref{lem:manifold_chart} gives $E$ the structure of smooth manifold without boundary.

	\newthought{Part 2. The maps $\Phi_\alpha$ define a smooth local trivialization}.
	Again, by construction, the maps $\varPhi_\alpha : \pi^{-1}(V_p) \to V_p\times\R^k$ are diffeomorphisms.
	Indeed, their coordinate representations with respect to the charts introduced above is just the identity\footnote{Exercise: expand the definitions and show that the following diagram commutes}:
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{tikzcd}
			{\pi^{-1}(V_p)} & {V_p\times\R^k} \\
			{\widetilde{V}_p\times\R^k} & {\widetilde{V}_p\times\R^k}
			\arrow["{\varPhi_\alpha}", from=1-1, to=1-2]
			\arrow["{\varphi_p\times\id_k}", from=1-2, to=2-2]
			\arrow["{\widetilde{\varphi}_p}"', from=1-1, to=2-1]
			\arrow["{\id_{n+k}}"', from=2-1, to=2-2]
		\end{tikzcd}.
	\end{equation}
	Since the sets $\pi^{-1}(V_p)$ with $V_p\subseteq U_\alpha$ cover $\pi^{-1}(U_\alpha)$, the bijection $\varPhi_\alpha:\pi^{-1}(U_\alpha)\to U_\alpha\times\R^k$ and its inverse are smooth, that is, $\varPhi_\alpha$ is a diffeomorphism.
	In a similar way, the coordinate representation of $\pi : E \to M$ is $\varphi_p \circ \pi \circ \widetilde{\varphi}_p^{-1} (x,v) = x$, so $\pi$ is smooth.
	Finally, $\Phi_\alpha$ satisfies all conditions to be a smooth local trivialization since $\varPhi_\alpha$ is linear by hypothesis and $\pi_1 \circ \varPhi_\alpha = \pi$, which follows from $\varPhi_\alpha(E_p) = \{p\}\times \R^k$.

	\newthought{Part 3. The smooth structure is unique}.
	Since the $\varPhi_\alpha$ have to be diffeomorphisms onto their images, any other atlas will have to contain the family of charts defined here, and therefore they must be in the same equivalence class, that is, in the same smooth structure.
\end{proof}

The previous exercise should already give you a hint at why property (iii) of the Theorem~\ref{thm:bundle_chart_thm} has such a strange form involving $k\times k$ invertible matrices.
The following lemma should clarify definitively why this is natural.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:transition_vb}
	Let $\pi:E \to M$ be a smooth vector bundle of rank $k$ over $M$. Let $U,V\subseteq M$, $U\cap V\neq \emptyset$.
	If $\varPhi : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times \R^k$ and $\Psi: \pi^{-1}(V) \to V \times \R^k$ are two smooth local trivializations of $E$, then there exists a smooth map $\tau: U\cap V \to GL(k, \R)$ such that
	\begin{align}
		\varPhi\circ\Psi^{-1} : (U\cap V)\times\R^k & \to \pi^{-1}(U\cap V) \to (U\cap V)\times \R^k \\
		(p,v)                                       & \mapsto (p, \tau(p) v).
	\end{align}
\end{lemma}

We saw in Example~\ref{ex:simple_bundles} that given two vector bundles on some manifolds there is a way to construct a product bundle out of them over the product of the manifolds.
In the special case of two bundles $E^1 \to M$ and $E^2 \to M$ over the same manifold, this would lead to $E^1 \times E^2 \to M \times M$.
It is natural to ask oneself if we can make a construction that combines multiple vector bundles over the same base space to a new bundle over that same base space.
This is called the Whitney sum of the bundles.
\begin{exercise}[Whitney sum]\label{ex:whitney}\idxdef{Whitney sum}
	Let $\pi^1 : E^1 \to M$ and $\pi^2 : E^2 \to M$ be two smooth vector bundles over $M$ of rank $k^1$ and $k^2$ respectively.
	The Whitney sum $E^1 \oplus E^2$ of $E^1$ and $E^2$ is the smooth vector bundle $\pi: E^1 \oplus E^2 \to M$ whose fibers
	\begin{equation}
		(E^1 \oplus E^2)_p = \pi^{-1}(p) =: (E^1_p \oplus E^2_p)
	\end{equation}
	are the direct sum of the fibers of the respective bundles, with the projection $\pi: (E^1\oplus E^2)_p \mapsto p$.

	Show, using Theorem~\ref{thm:bundle_chart_thm}, that $\pi: E^1 \oplus E^2 \to M$ defines a smooth vector bundle of rank $k^1 + k^2$.\\
	\textit{\small
		Hint: given two local trivializations $\varPhi$ and $\varPsi$ for $E^1\oplus E^2$, then $\varPhi\circ\varPsi^{-1}$ should have the form
		\begin{equation}
			\varPhi\circ\varPsi^{-1}(p, (v^1, v^2)) = (p, \tau(p)(v^1, v^2)), \quad
			\tau(p) :=
			\begin{pmatrix}
				\tau^1(p) & 0         \\
				0         & \tau^2(p)
			\end{pmatrix}.
		\end{equation}
		Here $\tau^1$ and $\tau^2$ denote transition functions of $E^1$ and $E^2$ respectively.
	}
\end{exercise}

\section{Sections of vector bundles}

\begin{marginfigure}
	\includegraphics{2_7-bdl_section.pdf}
	\caption{A useful mnemonic to remember what is a section, is to imagine it as a cross-section of the bundle.}
\end{marginfigure}

\begin{definition}\idxdef{Section (of a vector bundle)}
	A \emph{section} of a vector bundle $\pi:E \to M$ is a smooth map $S:M \to E$ such that $\pi\circ S = \id_M$. We denote the set of all sections of $E$ by $\Gamma(E)$.

	If, in the definition, $M$ is replaced by $U\subset M$, the section is called \emph{local section}. The set of local sections on $U\subset M$ is denoted $\Gamma(E|_U)$, where $E|_U := \pi^{-1}(U) = \{\eta\in E\;\mid\;\pi(\eta)\in U\} \subset E$ is the vector bundle obtained by restriction of the base of $E$ to $U$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	A careful look at the definition shows that vector fields are sections of $TM$, indeed $\fX(M) \equiv \Gamma(TM)$.
	This is a useful way to start understanding the bundle terminology: in some sense, sections of vector bundles are a generalisation of vector fields.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Vector fields in $\R^n$]
	If $E = M\times \R^r$, $M\subset\R^n$, then for any smooth map $F: M\to\R^r$ we have a section $S\in\Gamma(E)$ defined by $S(p) = (p, F(p))$. This is a classical euclidean vector field: a map that associates vectors to points (see Figure~\ref{fig:vectorfield-rn}).
	Notice, in particular, that functions $f\in C^\infty(M)$ are sections of the trivial bundle $M\times\R$:
	\begin{equation}
		\Gamma(M\times\R) \simeq C^\infty(M).
	\end{equation}
\end{example}

One can sometimes distinguish non--isomorphic bundles by looking at the complement of their zero section: since any vector bundle isomorphism $h:E_1\to E_2$ must map the zero section of $E_1$ onto the zero section of $E_2$, the complements of the zero sections in $E_1$ and $E_2$ must be homeomorphic.

	\includegraphics{2_9-vfield.pdf}
	\caption{A vector field ``attaches'' vectors to points.}%
	\label{fig:vectorfield-rn}
\end{marginfigure}

Even though, as we have seen, \emph{locally} $TM$ is diffeomorphic to $M\times\R^n$, this is not true in general with one exception.
\begin{exercise}\label{ex:trivializable}
	Let $M$ be a smooth $n$-manifold that can be covered by a single smooth chart.
	Show that $TM$ is diffeomorphic to $M\times\R^n$ (without applying Proposition~\ref{prop:trivializable}).
\end{exercise}

\begin{definition}\idxdef{Local frame}\idxdef{Global frame}
	A \emph{local frame} of a bundle $\pi:E\to M$ of rank $r$ is a family of $r$ local sections $\{S_1, \ldots, S_r\}\subset\Gamma(E|_U)$ such that $\{S_1(p), \ldots, S_r(p)\}$ is a basis for $E_p$ for all $p\in U$.
	If $U=M$ then $\{S_1, \ldots, S_r\}$ is called \emph{global frame}.
	Sometimes, the sections $S_j$ are called \emph{basis sections}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
	A chart on a $n$-manifold $M$ with local coordinates $(x^i)$ yields a local frame $\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial x^1}, \ldots, \frac{\partial}{\partial x^n}\right\}$ of the tangent bundle $TM$.
\end{example}

In the spirit of what we have seen about the previous example, we have the following proposition.

\begin{proposition}
	Let $\pi:E \to M$ be a smooth vector bundle of rank $k$.
	Let $X:M\to E$ be a map such that $\pi\circ X = \id_M$.
	If $\{S_i\}$ is a smooth local frame for $E$ over an open subset $U\subseteq M$, then $X$ is smooth on $U$ if and only if its component functions with respect to $\{S_i\}$ are smooth.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	Smoothness is a local property, so fix an arbitrary $q\in U$ and consider a neighbourhood $V\subseteq U$ of $q$ over which there is a local trivialization $\psi:\pi^{-1}(V)\to V\times\R^k$.

	Since $\psi$ is a diffeomorphism, $X$ is smooth on $V$ if and only if $\psi\circ X$ is smooth on $V$.
	Write $\psi\circ S_i(p) = (p, A(p)e_i)$, with $\{e_i\}$ the canonical basis of $\R^k$: the matrix $A(p)$ is invertible and depends smoothly on $p$ because the $S_i$ are smooth.
	If $\{X^i\}$ denote the component functions of $X$ with respect to $\{S_i\}$, then $\psi\circ X (p) = (p, A(p)(X^1(p), \ldots, X^k(p)))$ and, since matrix inversion is smooth on $GL(k,\R)$, $\psi\circ X$ is smooth if and only if the component functions $\{X^i\}$ are smooth.
\end{proof}

That is, given a local frame $\{S_1, \ldots, S_r\}\subset\Gamma(E|_U)$ of a vector bundle $\pi: E \to M$ we can express any section $X\in\Gamma(E)$ as a linear combination of elements of the frame:
\begin{equation}
	X = X^i S_i \quad\mbox{on }U,
\end{equation}
where $X^i\in C^\infty(U)$, $i=1,\ldots,r$.
Which was to be expected: after all, for each $p\in U\subset M$, the local frame is a basis for $E_p$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:trivializable}
	A vector bundle $\pi: E\to M$ is trivializable if and only if it admits a global frame.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	Let $\varphi: E \to M\times\R^r$ be a global trivialization and $\{e_1, \ldots, e_r\}$ the canonical basis for $\R^r$.
	For $p\in M$, $\{S_1(p), \ldots, S_r(p)\} := \left\{\varphi^{-1}(p, e_1), \ldots, \varphi^{-1}(p, e_r) \right\}$ is a global frame for $E$ (why?).

	Conversely, let $\{S_1, \ldots, S_r\}$ be a global frame for $E$. Then
	\begin{equation}
		\varphi: E \to M\times\R^r, \quad
		\left(p, v^i S_i(p)\right) \mapsto \left(p, (v^1, \ldots, v^r)\right),
	\end{equation}
	is a global trivialization for $E$.
	Indeed, $\varphi$ is a bijection since it is a linear isomorphism on each fiber and $\pi_1\circ\varphi = \pi$, and we only need to check that it is a diffeomorphism.

	Let $\psi:\pi^{-1}(V)\to V\times\R^r$ be any local trivialization and $A(p)$ the invertible matrix defined by $\psi\circ S_i(p) = (p, A(p)e_i)$, as in the proof of the previous proposition.
	Then
	\begin{align}
		\psi\circ\varphi^{-1}(p,v) &= \psi\left(v^iS_i(p)\right) = \big(p, A(p)v\big), \\
		\varphi\circ\psi^{-1}(p,w) &= \big(p, A(p)^{-1}w\big),
	\end{align}
	which are both smooth on $V\times\R^r$.
	Hence $\varphi = (\varphi\circ\psi^{-1})\circ\psi$ is smooth on $\pi^{-1}(V)$ and $\varphi^{-1} = \psi^{-1}\circ(\psi\circ\varphi^{-1})$ is smooth on $V\times\R^r$.
	Finally, since the sets $\pi^{-1}(V)$ cover $E$ and the sets $V\times\R^r$ cover $M\times\R^r$, both $\varphi$ and $\varphi^{-1}$ are smooth.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
	The cylinder $E = \bS^1\times\R$ is a trivializable vector bundle with $\pi:E\to \bS^1$.
	Incidentally, the cylinder is isomorphic to $T\bS^1$ (why?).
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}
	Let $M$ be a smooth $m$-manifold and $N$ a smooth $n$-manifold.
	Let $F:M\to N$ be an embedding and denote $\widetilde M = F(M)\subset N$.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Show that the tangent bundle of $M$ in $N$, given by $T\widetilde M := dF(TM) \subset TN\big|_{\widetilde M}$, is a subbundle of $TN\big|_{\widetilde M}$ by providing explicit local trivializations in terms of the charts $(U, \varphi)$ for $M$.
		\item Assume that there exists a smooth function $\Phi:N\to\R^{n-m}$ such that $\widetilde M := \{p\in N \mid \Phi(p) = 0\}$ and $d\Phi_p$ has full rank for all $p\in\widetilde M$. Prove that\footnote{Here $T\,N|_{\widetilde{M}}$ denotes the tangent bundle of $N$ restricted to the base points in $\widetilde{M}$.}
		      \begin{equation}
			      T\widetilde{M} = \left\{(p,v)\in T\,N|_{\widetilde{M}} \mid v\in\ker(d\Phi_p)\right\}.
		      \end{equation}
	\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
